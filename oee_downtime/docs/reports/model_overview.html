Section 1

Executive Summary

The machine health indicator model rates every machine on the shop floor each day as CRITICAL, ELEVATED or OK, based on how soon it is likely to experience an unplanned breakdown. The model was trained on three years of machine sensor data and maintenance records.

The model is intended to supplement the shop's current repair-interval maintenance method: when a machine nears its usual gap between repairs, an interval service replaces the wear components ahead of the failure. Over the quarter, the shop carried out 45 interval services, and 11 unplanned failures still occurred, resulting in 177 hours of unplanned downtime, almost all on machines that failed before their interval was reached.

The machine health indicator model ran in the background over the quarter, rating every machine daily with the ratings recorded and not acted on, so the machine failures occurred as they would have without it. This was necessary to validate the model's results. The model flagged CRITICAL ratings on 8 of the 11 unplanned failures on at least one of the 7 days before they occurred. Acting on those ratings would have turned 8 reactive repairs into planned services, avoiding about 85 hours of unplanned downtime in the quarter, worth about $44,000 a year in contribution margin. Additionally, as detailed in Section 3, the model greatly outperformed both baselines.

These results suggest this model is a valuable early-warning and maintenance prioritisation tool alongside the shop's existing repair-interval method.

In addition to the health indicator tier, the model lists the specific conditions that drove it, so the maintenance team can see why a machine was surfaced and what to inspect first. The signals that most heavily determine the indicator are listed below:

Section 2

Model Overview

Section 2.1

What This Model Does

The machine health indicator is built on a random forest machine learning algorithm. It answers one question for every machine on the floor, every day: which machines are likely to experience a breakdown soon?

1. What it watches
For each machine: daily sensor readings for vibration, bearing temperature, motor power, and hydraulic pressure, plus its age and utilization, recent alarms and downtime, where it stands in its repair interval and the time since its last calendar PM.
→
2. What it learns
From three years of history it learned the patterns that came before past breakdowns, such as vibration and heat creeping up, older machines failing sooner, and wear showing early in a machine's repair interval.
→
3. What it produces
A daily health indicator for every machine (CRITICAL, ELEVATED or OK) and the specific reasons behind it, delivered straight into the CMMS asset list.

The health indicator's tiers are described below:

Health indicatorWhat the model is sayingWhat the floor should do
CRITICALFailure likely within 7 daysSchedule maintenance now; treat as this week's priority.
ELEVATEDFailure likely within 8 to 21 daysPlan a service window in the next two to three weeks.
OKNo failure expected within 21 daysContinue the normal preventive-maintenance schedule.

The indicator is delivered in the shop's CMMS asset view: the fleet is ranked by health indicator and each machine carries its tier and the conditions driving it.

Section 2.2

Training Data Overview

The four condition-monitoring channels (spindle vibration, bearing temperature, spindle motor power and hydraulic pressure) are plotted below as monthly averages across all machines for the entire three-year training window. All four channels remain broadly steady over time: regular repairs and interval services account for most of the fluctuations seen over time, with the machine's readings then returning to baseline once completed.

Sensor Channels Over Three Years

The sensor channels look steady at the monthly level because the pre-failure spikes are short and machine-specific, so they average out across the fleet. The model instead picks up the anomaly readings that jump above a machine's own baseline.

Across all machines, monthly sensor anomaly activity rises and falls with the count of repairs. Looking at individual machines, average spindle vibration is quiet weeks out and climbs in the final days before a repair, and this is the strongest indicator of an impending machine breakdown. And the further a machine's readings sit above its own normal baseline, the sooner the next repair arrives, from about 15 days out when readings are normal to about 9 days when they are highly abnormal.

Section 3

Model Performance

Section 3.1

Scoring Summary

The model scores every machine-shift observation before the shift begins, and takes the higher of the day's two ratings. In January to March 2026, the model scored 1,848 observations covering 924 machine-days. It rated 102 machine-days CRITICAL (failure likely within 7 days), 118 ELEVATED (within 8 to 21 days) and 704 OK. This shop opens an unplanned repair within 7 days on about 7% of machine-shifts, and the fleet of 12 machines carries about 1.3 CRITICAL ratings on an average day.

Section 3.2

Accuracy and Validation

The model's accuracy and validation statistics are summarized below and presented in more detail in the ML technical report.

The shop's repair-interval maintenance process resolves most developing machine failures before they occur: in the scoring quarter, 45 interval services against 11 unplanned breakdowns. The model ran in the background over that entire quarter, its ratings recorded and not acted on, so that the record shows what it saw without changing what happened. To judge its accuracy and validate its results, we read it two ways:

  1. How many of the machines' unplanned breakdowns it rated CRITICAL in the 7 days before.
  2. What followed each of the model's CRITICAL ratings within 7 days: an unplanned repair (the warning was right and the machine broke down before its interval service), an interval service (the warning was right but the scheduled service reached the component first), or neither (the warning was wrong: a false alarm).

It is compared with two baselines: a rules baseline a planner could apply by hand, CRITICAL when a machine's 7-day alarm count is more than 1.5 times its usual level or its calendar PM is more than 14 days overdue; and the calendar PM schedule, CRITICAL when a PM is due within 7 days or overdue. Neither baseline reads where a machine stands in its repair interval. That's because the shop already acts on that through their current repair-interval maintenance schedule. The model does capture this repair interval data though, so its comparison to baseline shows what it adds beyond the signals the shop already had.

The model flagged a CRITICAL rating ahead of 8 of the quarter's 11 unplanned machine failures, against 6 of 11 for the rules baseline and 3 of 11 for the calendar PM baseline. The three failures the model did not flag were two operator-induced failures (MCH-003 on 5 January and MCH-010 on 7 January), which give no sensor or alarm warning, and a tooling failure on MCH-001 on 26 February, one day before its interval service was due, where the model's 7-day probability peaked at 0.13, under the 0.15 threshold for CRITICAL.

Unplanned Machine Failures Flagged as Critical, Model vs. Baselines

False alarms occur when the model or a baseline method flags a CRITICAL rating when nothing occurs. This indicates low precision, and a higher percentage of false alarms means the flags can't be trusted. Only 25% of the model's CRITICAL flags were false alarms, compared to 70% for the rules baseline and 65% for the calendar PM baseline. The model also raised CRITICAL flags on fewer days (102) than the rules baseline (131) or calendar PM baseline (203), further validating its reliability.

What Followed Each CRITICAL Rating, 7-Day Window

Ratings followed by an interval service are warnings the method resolved; ratings followed by neither are false alarms. The three parts are exclusive and sum to the rated total.

The chart below shows the same information, but for both CRITICAL and ELEVATED flags, across a 21 day period. The rules baseline warned ahead of more of the quarter's failures (9 of 11 against 8), on more than twice as many rated days (474 against 220) with 30% followed by nothing against 8%.

What Followed Each CRITICAL or ELEVATED Rating, 21-Day Window

ROC-AUC is a measure of how accurate the model's predictions were. It compares the machine-shift windows that ended in a machine failure against ones that didn't and examines whether the predictions differed between the two. A score of 0.5 means there was no difference, 1.0 means the predictions always captured the machine failure. As shown below, the model was much more accurate in the 7-day CRITICAL window (0.79, versus 0.58 and 0.45 for the baselines) versus the 21-day CRITICAL and ELEVATED window (0.62, versus 0.59 and 0.45 for the baselines).

Section 3.4

Limitations

Being clear about the model's limits is what makes it usable. It is an early-warning aid with a wide net, running beside a method that already pre-empts most wear-out failures.

  • It ranks how soon, not how severe or how costly. The output is how soon a failure is likely, not how serious the repair will be or what it will cost.
  • Most of its warnings precede a service that was already due. Wear looks the same on the sensors whether it ends in an interval service or a failure, and the service comes first 72% of the time. 55 of the quarter's 102 CRITICAL machine-days were followed within 7 days by an interval service and no failure, and 21 by a failure. A CRITICAL rating on a machine whose service is days away adds little; on a machine early in its interval it is the signal worth acting on.
  • One machine carries much of the alerting. MCH-011 is serviced every 7 days and was rated CRITICAL on 49 of its 77 operating days in the quarter.
  • Gradual failures are easier than sudden ones. Wear-driven mechanical problems announce themselves through rising vibration and heat: a CRITICAL day came in the 7 days before 6 of the 6 mechanical failures across the test and scoring periods. Operator-induced failures leave no such trace: 1 of 3.
  • The counts are small. The scoring quarter has 11 unplanned failures and the test period 16. A difference of two or three failures between the indicator and a simple rule is within what a different quarter could reverse.
  • It is decision support, not automation. The model ranks and explains; a person still decides what work to schedule. It is designed to inform maintenance judgement, not replace it.
  • It stays current through monitoring. Machine behaviour drifts over time, so the model is watched every month and retrained when the monitoring rules call for it, as detailed in the monitoring report.
