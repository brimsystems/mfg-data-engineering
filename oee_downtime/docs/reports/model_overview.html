Section 1

Executive Summary

The machine health indicator rates every machine on the shop floor each day as CRITICAL, ELEVATED or OK, by how soon it is likely to need an unplanned repair, so that the work can be planned before a breakdown happens. The model was trained on three years of machine sensor data and maintenance records. Over the past three months it has been live, rating machine health daily.

During this time, the shop logged 44 unplanned failures and 625 hours of unplanned downtime. The health indicator showed CRITICAL on at least one of the 7 days before 91% of these failures (40 of 44). The comparison that matters is the interval baseline, a rule that flags a machine when the time since its last repair nears that machine's usual gap between repairs: it reached 82%. The calendar PM schedule, the shop's current practice, reached 30%. Over the same three months, when the indicator read CRITICAL a repair opened within 7 days 67% of the time (precision), and it read CRITICAL on 75% of the machine-days that had a repair within 7 days (recall), against 65% and 67% for the interval baseline.

Downtime and Failures with a CRITICAL Day Before: Calendar PM, Interval Baseline and Health Indicator

Acting on a CRITICAL rating before the failure could have avoided an estimated 228 hours of unplanned downtime in Q1 (assuming a 40% reduction in downtime for preventive versus reactive maintenance), worth about $116,000 in annualized contribution margin. The interval baseline, on the same assumption, gives 199 hours and $103,000. The difference, 29 hours in the quarter and about $13,000 a year, is the model's contribution over a rule the shop could run without it.

Section 2

Model Overview

Section 2.1

What This Model Does

The health indicator is built on XGBoost, a gradient-boosted decision-tree algorithm. It answers one question for every machine on the floor, every day: which machines are likely to need an unplanned repair soon, and how soon? It does not diagnose a specific fault or generate a repair order on its own, but rather serves as an early-warning and maintenance prioritisation tool.

1. What it watches
For each machine: daily sensor readings for vibration, bearing temperature, motor power, and hydraulic pressure, plus its age and utilization, recent alarms and downtime, and time since the last breakdown and last service.
→
2. What it learns
From three years of history it learned the patterns that came before past breakdowns, such as vibration and heat creeping up, older machines failing sooner, and risk rising soon after a repair.
→
3. What it produces
A daily health indicator for every machine (CRITICAL, ELEVATED or OK) and the specific reasons behind it, delivered straight into the CMMS asset list.

The health indicator's tiers are described below:

Health indicatorWhat the model is sayingWhat the floor should do
CRITICALFailure likely within 7 daysSchedule maintenance now; treat as this week's priority.
ELEVATEDFailure likely within 8 to 21 daysPlan a service window in the next two to three weeks.
OKNo failure expected within 21 daysContinue the normal preventive-maintenance schedule.

The indicator is delivered where the maintenance team already works. Section 2.2

Training Data Overview

The four condition-monitoring channels (vibration, bearing temperature, motor power, and hydraulic pressure) are plotted below as monthly fleet averages across the full training window, each with a fitted trend line. Three of the four sit flat at their baseline, which is what a healthy fleet should look like; spindle vibration is the exception, drifting steadily upward as the fleet ages. That slow rise reflects background wear that the model reads underneath the sharper pre-failure spikes.

Sensor Channels Over Three Years

The channels look calm in aggregate because the pre-failure spikes are short and machine-specific, so they average out across the fleet. The model instead picks up the anomalies, readings that jump above a machine's own baseline.

The three charts below show the importance of these anomaly readings. Across the fleet, monthly anomaly activity rises and falls with the actual unplanned-failure count and tends to lead it. Zooming into individual machines, average spindle vibration is quiet weeks out and climbs steadily in the final days before an unplanned repair. And the further a machine's readings sit above its own normal baseline, the sooner the next unplanned repair tends to arrive, from about 25 days out when readings are normal to about 9 days when they are highly abnormal. Together these confirm the condition-monitoring sensors as the model's leading indicators.

Looking at the unplanned breakdown data broken out by failure mode reveals that tooling and mechanical problems make up about three-quarters (76%) of all unplanned failures. These wear-driven modes are the ones that the condition-monitoring sensors' data reveal, and so are the ones driving the model's predictive power. The electrical, operator-induced, and environmental modes are less common and give far less warning.

Section 3

Model Performance

Section 3.1

Scoring Summary

Every machine-shift observation is scored before the shift begins, and each machine takes the higher of its day's two ratings. In January to March 2026, the model scored 1,848 observations covering 924 machine-days. It rated 274 machine-days CRITICAL (failure likely within 7 days), 317 ELEVATED (within 8 to 21 days) and 333 OK. This shop opens an unplanned repair within 7 days on about 25% of machine-days, so a fleet of 12 machines carries about 4 CRITICAL ratings on an average day.

The model learned on data from January 2023 to December 2024, was tuned and calibrated on January to August 2025, and was then scored once on the held-out test set. Three candidate algorithms, a logistic regression, a random forest, and a gradient-boosted XGBoost model, were each tuned over 100 Optuna trials per window and compared on validation average precision. XGBoost was the strongest, at 0.855 averaged across the two windows against 0.786 for the logistic regression, 0.850 for the random forest, and was carried forward.

Without the ten sensor features the 7-day model reaches ROC-AUC 0.87 against 0.92 with them; the interval rule reaches 0.79.

Event-level view: was a failure preceded by a CRITICAL day?

The window measures above are averages across every machine-day. For preventive maintenance the question is narrower: when a machine is about to fail, was it rated CRITICAL in time to act? Of the 44 unplanned failures in the Q1 scoring window, the health indicator showed CRITICAL on at least one of the 7 days before 40 of them (91%), the interval baseline before 36 (82%) and the calendar PM schedule before 13 (30%). These are the failures the downtime estimate in the executive summary is built on.

Section 3.4

What It Can and Cannot Predict

Being clear about the model's limits is what makes it trustworthy. It is a strong early-warning aid, not a crystal ball, and it is deliberately honest about the failures it cannot see coming.

  • It ranks how soon, not how severe or how costly. The output is how soon a failure is likely, not how serious the repair will be or what it will cost.
  • Gradual failures are easier than sudden ones. Wear-driven mechanical problems announce themselves through rising vibration and heat: a CRITICAL day came in the 7 days before 90% of mechanical failures across the test and scoring windows. Operator-induced failures leave no such trace and are the mode the indicator catches least, at 62%.
  • A quiet reading is not a guarantee. Roughly a third of real failures give no clear sensor precursor. Those machines still receive an ELEVATED heads-up from age and history, but not always a tight CRITICAL alert, so the preventive-maintenance schedule remains the safety net.
  • It is decision support, not automation. The model ranks and explains; a person still decides what work to schedule. It is designed to inform maintenance judgement, not replace it.
  • It stays current through monitoring. Machine behaviour drifts over time, so the model is watched continuously and retrained when its accuracy slips, as detailed in the monitoring report.
