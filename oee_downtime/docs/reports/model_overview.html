Section 1

Executive Summary

The shop runs a repair-interval method: when a machine nears its usual gap between repairs, an interval service replaces the wear components ahead of the failure. In the scoring quarter the method carried out 45 interval services, and 11 unplanned failures still occurred, 177 hours of unplanned downtime, almost all on machines that failed before their interval was reached. The health indicator ran in shadow mode over the quarter, rating every machine daily with the ratings recorded and not acted on, so the quarter's failures occurred as they would have without it. It read CRITICAL on at least one of the 7 days before 8 of the 11 failures (7 of the 8 that were not operator error), by reading wear as it develops and where it falls in the machine's interval; the method acts on the interval alone. Acting on those ratings would have turned 8 reactive repairs into planned services, avoiding about 85 hours of unplanned downtime in the quarter (planned work runs at about a third of a reactive repair's duration, as the shop's interval services do), worth about $44,000 a year in contribution margin. The net is wide: about one CRITICAL machine-day in five is followed by a failure within 7 days, and 58 of the quarter's 102 CRITICAL days fell in the week before a service already scheduled.

Scored shift by shift over the same three months, when the indicator read CRITICAL an unplanned repair opened within 7 days 18% of the time (precision), and it read CRITICAL on 30% of the machine-shifts that had an unplanned repair within 7 days (recall).

Section 2

Model Overview

Section 2.1

What This Model Does

The health indicator is built on random forest, an ensemble of decision trees. It answers one question for every machine on the floor, every day: which machines are likely to need an unplanned repair soon, and how soon? It does not diagnose a specific fault or generate a repair order on its own, but rather serves as an early-warning and maintenance prioritisation tool beside the repair-interval method and the calendar PM.

1. What it watches
For each machine: daily sensor readings for vibration, bearing temperature, motor power, and hydraulic pressure, plus its age and utilization, recent alarms and downtime, where it stands in its repair interval and the time since its last calendar PM.
→
2. What it learns
From three years of history it learned the patterns that came before past breakdowns, such as vibration and heat creeping up, older machines failing sooner, and wear showing early in a machine's repair interval.
→
3. What it produces
A daily health indicator for every machine (CRITICAL, ELEVATED or OK) and the specific reasons behind it, delivered straight into the CMMS asset list.

The health indicator's tiers are described below:

Health indicatorWhat the model is sayingWhat the floor should do
CRITICALFailure likely within 7 daysSchedule maintenance now; treat as this week's priority.
ELEVATEDFailure likely within 8 to 21 daysPlan a service window in the next two to three weeks.
OKNo failure expected within 21 daysContinue the normal preventive-maintenance schedule.

The indicator is delivered where the maintenance team already works. Section 2.2

Training Data Overview

The four condition-monitoring channels (vibration, bearing temperature, motor power, and hydraulic pressure) are plotted below as monthly fleet averages across the full training window, each with a fitted trend line. Three of the four sit flat at their baseline, which is what a healthy fleet should look like; spindle vibration is the exception, drifting steadily upward as the fleet ages. That slow rise reflects background wear that the model reads underneath the sharper pre-failure spikes.

Sensor Channels Over Three Years

The channels look calm in aggregate because the pre-failure spikes are short and machine-specific, so they average out across the fleet. The model instead picks up the anomalies, readings that jump above a machine's own baseline.

The three charts below show what these readings lead. The wear a sensor picks up ends in one of two ways: an interval service, when the machine reaches its repair interval first, or an unplanned repair, when it does not. The charts therefore measure each reading against the machine's next repair of either kind. Across the fleet, monthly anomaly activity rises and falls with the count of repairs. Zooming into individual machines, average spindle vibration is quiet weeks out and climbs in the final days before a repair. And the further a machine's readings sit above its own normal baseline, the sooner the next repair arrives, from about 15 days out when readings are normal to about 9 days when they are highly abnormal. Against unplanned repairs alone the same readings separate far less, because about four in five of the failures the wear leads to are pre-empted by an interval service; this is the main limit on the indicator and is set out in Section 3.

Section 3

Model Performance

Section 3.1

Scoring Summary

Every machine-shift observation is scored before the shift begins, and each machine takes the higher of its day's two ratings. In January to March 2026, the model scored 1,848 observations covering 924 machine-days. It rated 102 machine-days CRITICAL (failure likely within 7 days), 118 ELEVATED (within 8 to 21 days) and 704 OK. This shop opens an unplanned repair within 7 days on about 7% of machine-shifts, and the fleet of 12 machines carries about 1.3 CRITICAL ratings on an average day.

The model learned on data from January 2023 to December 2024, was tuned and calibrated on January to August 2025, and was then scored once on the held-out test set. Three candidate algorithms, a logistic regression, a random forest, and a gradient-boosted XGBoost model, were each tuned over 100 Optuna trials per window and compared on validation average precision. The margin between the first two is narrow.

Without the ten sensor features the 7-day model reaches ROC-AUC 0.70 on the test set against 0.77 with them.

Section 3.4

What It Can and Cannot Predict

Being clear about the model's limits is what makes it usable. It is an early-warning aid with a wide net, running beside a method that already pre-empts most wear-out failures.

  • It ranks how soon, not how severe or how costly. The output is how soon a failure is likely, not how serious the repair will be or what it will cost.
  • Most of its warnings precede a service that was already due. Wear looks the same on the sensors whether it ends in an interval service or a failure, and about four times in five the service comes first. 58 of the quarter's 102 CRITICAL machine-days fell in the week before an interval service and 21 in the week before a failure. A CRITICAL rating on a machine whose service is days away adds little; on a machine early in its interval it is the signal worth acting on.
  • One machine carries much of the alerting. MCH-011 is serviced every 7 days and was rated CRITICAL on 49 of its 77 operating days in the quarter.
  • Gradual failures are easier than sudden ones. Wear-driven mechanical problems announce themselves through rising vibration and heat: a CRITICAL day came in the 7 days before 6 of the 6 mechanical failures across the test and scoring periods. Operator-induced failures leave no such trace: 1 of 3.
  • The counts are small. The scoring quarter has 11 unplanned failures and the test period 16. A difference of two or three failures between the indicator and a simple rule is within what a different quarter could reverse.
  • It is decision support, not automation. The model ranks and explains; a person still decides what work to schedule. It is designed to inform maintenance judgement, not replace it.
  • It stays current through monitoring. Machine behaviour drifts over time, so the model is watched every month and retrained when the monitoring rules call for it, as detailed in the monitoring report.
