This report details the findings from the shop's new job costing functionality. Presented below is analysis of job margins, job cost overruns vs. estimates, blended vs. work-center pool rates, job cost overrun flags, repricing opportunities, and the characteristics of jobs with negative margins.

Section 1

Job Margin Overview

In 2025, the shop earned $56.1M of revenue from 4,355 jobs. It earned $14.2M of gross margin (revenue less variable production costs and overhead), a 25.4% rate. This actual margin was 3.4 percentage points lower than the 28.8% rate the shop's estimates promised as actual job costs came in $852K over the jobs' estimates.

At the job-level, the average margin was 21.2% in 2025 against the 26.8% estimates. Larger jobs carry a higher margin, which is why the shop's total margin was 4.2 percentage points higher than the job-level average. In the distribution below, we see that 2,390 jobs (55% of total) had margins above the 21.2% average and 1,965 jobs (45%) were below it, while 322 jobs (7.4%) lost money. The second chart shows each job's actual margin less its estimated margin: 63% of jobs came in below their estimate, by a median of 9 points, and the red bars are the 13% more than 20 points below.

Jobs on repeat parts carry a higher average margin than new quoted work (23.2% versus 19.1%). At the same time, jobs on repeat parts missed their margin estimates more frequently than new quoted work (68.8% vs 53.0%). There are significant margin differences between jobs of different sizes. Jobs with lots under 25 pieces average 13.6% a job and lose money on 19.3% of them; lots of 25 to 100 pieces average 22.4% and lose money on 5.7%, and lots over 100 pieces average 24.2% and lose money on 2.6%. The small lots also miss their estimates by the most, an average of 11.6 points against 4.7 and 2.9. The primary reason for this is that setup hours, which are consistent across jobs, have a larger impact on small jobs' lower revenue.

What the Blended Rate Hid

The ERP costed every labor hour at one blended shop rate, $178 in 2025 and $185 in 2026. The work-center pools, as set in May 2026, run from $102 an hour at deburr to $277 at the 5-axis cell. Costed at the pools, the families that run on the manual and secondary cells gain (Fixtures and tooling +6.6 points, Weldments and assemblies +6.5) and the families that run on the expensive cells lose (Aerospace brackets -7.4, Turbine components -2.9). Aerospace brackets is the shop's best family at the blended rate and number 8 of 11 at the pools. The blended rate left $902K of cost out of the 2 families it underpriced, $1.15M at the markup price, and overstated the cost of the other 9 by $1.41M (96% of the cost measured). This is what the "labor rate" movements in the repricing queue are.

Section 4

Jobs Caught in Progress

The job cost screen flags a job while it is open: at the first operation where its labor hours to date exceed the estimate to date by more than 15% and by at least two hours, or at the first material issue where material exceeds the estimate by more than 15% and $150. The flag is replayed here over the 4,355 jobs completed from the 2025 releases, to show what the shop could have known and when.

The Flag Replayed over the 2025 Jobs

1,064 jobs would have been flagged, 1,014 of them with operations still to run and 494 with operations left and 3 or more days before the job shipped (96% of the cost measured). Those 494 jobs ran $1.06M over their estimates, and $308K of it (29%) came after the flag: on operations that started after it, and on material issued and outside processing received after it. That is the part a warning could have changed.

A flag allows four things, none of them automatic. Check the routing standard and the program before the next operation runs. Move the remaining operations to another machine. Call the customer about the quantity, the delivery or a change order. Or finish the job knowing what it will cost.

Section 5

Repricing

Exposure. At today's material prices, pool rates and measured standards, 262 of the 1,600 repeat parts (16%) have a standing price below current cost plus the shop's standard 28% markup (the markup price), carrying 11% of repeat revenue. The gap is modest on most of them: a median of 5.1%, with nine in ten under 17%; 4 sit below cost outright. At current volume the exposure is $236K a year, and the own-product line adds $355K: together 1.1% of 2025 revenue. The gap is what the annual across-the-board letters (3.0% to 3.5% a year) missed on the parts whose inputs moved most: since the last quote, current cost rose a median 22% on the parts below the markup price against 1% on the rest, while the letters raised the standing prices of those parts a median 9%.

What is defensible. In the shop's experience, as the owner and the controller described it, a routine annual increase of 3 to 5% is accepted without discussion. A larger increase can land selectively, part by part, when the cost driver is documented, and most often that driver is material passed through at what it now costs. Beyond that the relationship risk is real, and the owner decides.

Decisions. The controller and the owner decided every part below the markup price in weeks 7 to 9 on that basis: a gap within the routine range was repriced; a larger gap was repriced where material or outside processing drove it and the movement could be shown part by part, and held otherwise with the reason recorded; a large gap on a small part was exited, and on the 17 largest programs the increase was taken in two steps, half now and the balance at the blanket renewal.

Pricing the Release

A standing price is set for a quoted lot, and a blanket release is whatever the customer calls off. In 2025, 72 of the 2,503 repeat-part releases (2.9%) were below half the lot the price was quoted at. The setup is the same whatever the release, so those jobs carried it over fewer pieces than the price assumed: they earned 16.0% against 27.8% on the other repeat releases, and 39% of them lost money. Charging the part of the estimated setup the price did not cover, (1 less released over quoted) times the estimated setup hours at the pool rate, would have added $22K to those releases and taken them to 20.5% (96% of the cost measured). The action is a setup line on any release below half the quoted lot, or a minimum lot charge on the standing price.

Section 6

The Jobs That Lost Money

322 jobs lost money in 2025, $224,228 in total (95% of the cost measured). Set against their estimates, the losses are mostly not a pricing story: only 32 of the 322 were estimated to lose money before they started. The other 290 were estimated to make money, a median estimated margin of 22%, and lost it in the job; they carry 74% of the loss. Each job carries the driver the reporting layer assigns by rule, the same rules the Job Variance report applies to every job, and the action the driver maps to: correct the routing standard, correct the quote, bill the change order, reprice the part, fix the process, or accept it as a one-off. This is the list the owner and estimator work from, and it matches what they see in the ERP.
